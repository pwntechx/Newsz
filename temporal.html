Velocity, Gravity, and the Projection Equations

The standard equations are not wrong. Within conventional physics, time travel remains effectively impossible because the required conditions cannot be produced at macroscopic scale. The 5D interpretive model treats that impossibility as applying to the method — not necessarily to the ontology.

Special Relativity: Δt' = Δt / √(1 − v²/c²)
→ As velocity approaches light speed, the moving observer's clock slows relative to a stationary observer's clock.
General Relativity: Δt' = Δt · √(1 − 2GM/rc²)
→ Closer to a massive body (smaller r), time runs slower. GPS satellites correct for this effect daily.

Where Δt = time interval for stationary observer · Δt' = time interval for moving or gravitationally displaced observer · v = velocity · c = speed of light · G = gravitational constant · M = mass of body · r = distance from center of mass.

§ 04 — REMOTE VIEWING AND GOVERNMENT PROGRAMS

Weak Signals Held Open · The Institutional Record

If an observer can index to a non-local coordinate — if the rendering frame is not strictly bound to the physical position of the body — then anomalous perception of spatially or temporally remote information might be the earliest, weakest detectable signal of that capacity. This section documents the institutional record of attempts to investigate that possibility.

These programs are held as weak signal. Not evidence that remote viewing works. Not evidence that it doesn't. Evidence that serious people with serious resources held the question open long enough to fund systematic investigation — and that the results were anomalous enough to prevent clean closure.

CIA / STARGATE PROGRAM · 1972–1995 DOCUMENTED · DECLASSIFIED

The CIA funded remote viewing research at Stanford Research Institute under physicists Russell Targ and Hal Puthoff. Subjects were asked to describe geographically remote locations with no conventional access to information about them. The program ran for over two decades, was funded by the Senate Intelligence Committee, and involved the DIA (Defense Intelligence Agency) in operational applications. The 1995 review by the American Institutes for Research found statistically anomalous results above chance in controlled conditions — and also found the effect was not operationally reliable enough to justify continued funding. The program was terminated. Both findings are real. Neither cancels the other.

INGO SWANN · COORDINATE REMOTE VIEWING PROTOCOL DOCUMENTED · CONTESTED

Ingo Swann, working with SRI, developed the Coordinate Remote Viewing (CRV) protocol — a structured method for accessing remote spatial information using only geographic coordinates as a target anchor. His documented sessions included a 1973 experiment at Stanford in which he described the interior of a shielded room he had never entered. The experiment was witnessed and documented by physicists. The result was anomalous. It was not replicated under conditions that closed the methodological questions. Swann's theoretical model held that consciousness is not localized to the brain — that attention can index to non-local coordinates. This is consistent with the 5D frame. It is not evidence for it.

JOE McMONEAGLE · OPERATIONAL REMOTE VIEWER DOCUMENTED · CONTESTED

McMoneagle served as the Army's operational remote viewer (Warrant Officer 341) and was awarded the Legion of Merit for intelligence provided through remote viewing. His operational record includes documented sessions on Soviet military installations, hostage locations, and submarine positions. Specific claims were confirmed after the fact in several cases. The methodology was not double-blind in all operational applications, making causal attribution difficult. He is the most decorated operational remote viewer in the program's history. The award citation is a matter of public record.

PRINCETON ENGINEERING ANOMALIES RESEARCH LAB · PEAR · 1979–2007 WEAK SIGNAL · NOT REPLICATED

The PEAR lab at Princeton investigated whether human intention could influence random physical systems — specifically random number generators. Over 28 years, the lab reported a cumulative statistical effect above chance. The effect size was small (roughly 1 part in 10,000 above random). Independent replication attempts have not consistently reproduced the effect under tighter controls. PEAR is held here as weak signal — anomalous enough to note, not robust enough to count as evidence. The distinction between "anomalous" and "evidence" is the Glass Box discipline applied to this node.
